# Fix round trips before chasing expert offload

Colibri will not reduce the NetBox agent's latency and should be skipped: it exists to run models that do not fit in RAM by streaming experts from NVMe, whereas the agent's 105 GiB Qwen3.8-Flash-Next already sits in 376 GB of RAM with every expert on CPU via `-ncmoe 48`, and colibri's own published figure for this exact model is **1.9–4.0 tok/s on CPU** against the **13.5 tok/s median** the agent decodes at today. The wider family of expert-offloading tools (GPU expert caches, learned prefetchers, KTransformers-style CPU kernels) attacks decode only, and decode is at most about half of a warm turn: a question costs **~5.6 sequential LLM calls**, each re-sending an **~8.7k-token fixed prefix** that is **~85% served from llama-server's prefix cache**, with the remaining time split between prefilling new tool-result text at ~130 tok/s and decoding at 13.5 tok/s, so even a hypothetical 1.5x decode gain trims a typical turn by roughly 15–25% and leaves the worst turns (an 80 s cold prefix, a 167 s prefill of a 20k-token tool result) untouched. The single offloading feature worth a measured trial is upstream llama.cpp's `--moe-cache-mib` (merged 2026-10-07, absent from the local 1af554f8f build), and it is hemmed in: the PR recommends a cache of ~10% of expert bytes (~7 GiB here), the box has ~3.3 GiB of VRAM headroom across two PCIe 3.0 cards, and the PR itself warns of diminishing returns on PCIe 3.0. KTransformers needs AMX, which the Cascade Lake Xeon Silver 4210 lacks; ik_llama.cpp, MoE-Infinity, PowerInfer, Fiddler and the academic prefetchers either cannot run the `qwen4_exp` architecture or cannot run on Turing. The levers with the largest measured payoff are on the agent and server-config side: fewer round trips per question (one GraphQL introspection replaced 19 MCP calls; rejecting `__in` cut a session from 1,552 s to 1,312 s), smaller tool results, a GPU-to-NUMA-node affinity check that the dual-socket evidence says can halve prefill, `kernel.numa_balancing=0`, and slot save/restore so switching conversations stops paying an 80 s cold prefill.

## A turn is 5.6 serial calls, each half prefill and half decode

The agent's own traces fix where the minutes go. Across the full 90-question benchmark the model handled **507 requests at a 13.47 tok/s median decode**, i.e. 5.6 LLM calls per question, with wall time of 6 h 47 m or **271.6 s per question** ([rtf local-frontier-inference.md](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/rtf-research/blob/main/docs/phases/phase-5-production-deepagents/local-frontier-inference.md); [oDA kvvram-full trace](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/docs/traces/2026-09-23_netbox-benchmark-v5_kvvram-full.md)). In a real seven-turn web session, **model generation took 97.9% of 1,361.5 s**, NetBox tool execution 2.0%, and all five middleware wrappers together 0.19% ([rtf ADR-0039](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/rtf-research/blob/main/docs/adr/0039-web-serving-layer-serialised-turns-server-token-counts.md)). Nothing outside the model is worth optimising.

Inside the model, the split is bimodal. The cleanest single-turn datapoint is the Gate-3 turn "List the sites for tenant Dunder-Mifflin": 167.2 s, 4 model calls, **37,387 tokens read of which 26,862 were cache hits and 778 written**, with prefill at 122.3 tok/s and decode at 14.3–14.9 tok/s ([oDA 2026-09-28_web-chat.md](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/docs/development/2026-09-28_web-chat.md)). Working that through, the 10,525 uncached tokens cost about 86 s of prefill and the 778 output tokens about 54 s of decode, so prefill was roughly half the turn and decode a third; but 8,743 of those prefilled tokens were the cold first call, so on a warm follow-up in the same thread the prefill share collapses to under 2k tokens and decode becomes the larger half. The author's earlier "two thirds decode" estimate was made when decode ran at 4–5 tok/s with `--no-kv-offload`; removing that flag doubled decode to ~10.8 tok/s and the split has not been re-measured since ([rtf RESEARCH_LOG 2026-09-21](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/rtf-research/blob/main/RESEARCH_LOG.md); [rtf ADR-0038](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/rtf-research/blob/main/docs/adr/0038-local-frontier-model-viable-cost-not-capability.md)).

The outliers are all prefill. A cold first call after a restart or a conversation switch pays the full prefix: **81.8 s for 8,743 tokens** ([oDA docs/setup/web-chat.md](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/docs/setup/web-chat.md)). A 64,199-character tool result became **19,976 new tokens prefilled at 119.6 tok/s, about 167 s of silence**, and the `device_id__in` bug produced a 283 s model call ([oDA 2026-09-28_web-chat.md](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/docs/development/2026-09-28_web-chat.md)). And the number of calls itself is noisy: the same advanced question under identical configuration took **9 LLM calls in one run and 14 in another**, with tool execution only 5.4 s of 413.9 s ([rtf ADR-0038](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/rtf-research/blob/main/docs/adr/0038-local-frontier-model-viable-cost-not-capability.md)). Five extra calls at 30–40 s each is more than any offloading technique in this review could save on the calls that remain.

This profile sets the ceiling for everything that follows. Expert caching, prefetching and faster expert kernels act on decode, and in llama.cpp the new expert cache is explicitly bypassed for batches above 32 tokens, so it does nothing for prefill ([PR #29887](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/29887)). By Amdahl's arithmetic on the Gate-3 numbers, a 1.5x decode speedup saves about 18 s of a 167 s turn; on a warm, small-result turn it saves more like a quarter. Worth having, but not the first thing to reach for.

## Colibri solves a problem this box does not have

Colibri is a 3.5-month-old pure-C engine whose premise is that "the routed experts stay on the disk and are read when the router asks for them, through a cache that learns which experts your work uses" ([colibri README](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri/blob/main/README.md)). Its headline is GLM-5.2's 744B parameters on a 25 GB laptop. That is the opposite regime from the NetBox box, where the whole 105 GiB model is RAM-resident and the only question is how fast ~6B active parameters per token can be pulled out of DDR4. Colibri itself says as much about its two modes: for models that fit RAM it keeps "all 10,240 experts live in RAM, the hot ones are promoted into DEVICE_LOCAL VRAM", which is the same design space as llama.cpp `-ncmoe` plus a GPU cache ([qwen36-cuda-tier.md](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri/blob/main/docs/qwen36-cuda-tier.md)).

In that overlapping regime colibri loses to llama.cpp by its own measurements. On an RTX 3070 with Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, colibri's best configuration reaches **30.0 tok/s where Ollama (llama.cpp underneath) does 38.1 tok/s on the same card**, and at equal bits llama.cpp's Q4_K_M scores better perplexity (7.147) than colibri's gs64 int4 (7.325) ([qwen36-cuda-tier.md](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri/blob/main/docs/qwen36-cuda-tier.md); [qwen36.md](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri/blob/main/docs/qwen36.md)). For the agent's actual model, colibri's README reports **Qwen3.8-Flash-Next at 1.91–2.56 tok/s with 32–96 experts per layer cached, 3.99 tok/s with int4 experts, and a 512-token prompt taking 30–44 s** on its reference 8-core machine ([colibri README](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri/blob/main/README.md)). The agent already decodes 3–7x faster and prefills at ~130 tok/s.

The practical obstacles stack on top. Colibri's native format is safetensors; in-place GGUF reading only landed on the `dev` branch on 2026-10-17, is CPU/Vulkan only, and "CUDA takes no GGUF in any engine, the Qwens' included" ([PR #2140](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri/pull/2140)). The Vulkan path's staged-upload mode for cards without Resizable BAR, which covers "every Turing card", "has not been measured on a card without Resizable BAR"; two-GPU Vulkan was "checked on Lavapipe opened twice; two real GPUs not measured yet" ([CHANGELOG.md](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). Its API docs warn that a large system prompt and tool catalog produce "a long silent wait before the first token", `tool_choice: required` is prompt-level only, and the engines "do not agree on how" forced tool choice is applied ([api.md](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri/blob/main/docs/api.md)). The project ships ~1,000 commits a month with a single maintainer at 2,050 commits, has logged release-to-release regressions such as an 18% decode loss in v1.5.0, and independent reviewers call it "a research toy and a proof of concept, not a daily driver" ([GitHub commits API](https://api.github.com/repos/JustVugg/colibri/commits?sha=main); [issues search](https://api.github.com/search/issues?q=repo:JustVugg/colibri+type:issue&sort=comments); [modelfit.io](https://modelfit.io/blog/run-744b-model-25gb-ram-honest-math/)).

Two of colibri's ideas are still worth noting as ideas rather than as software. Its persisted routing-heat warmstart (`.coli_usage`) and the finding that "profile quality matters more than raw VRAM capacity: the same 150 GB tier measured 0.94–1.64 tok/s hot-first vs 0.29 tok/s filled without routing heat" are evidence that a narrow, repetitive workload like NetBox queries skews expert usage enough for a small cache to matter ([cuda.md](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri/blob/main/docs/cuda.md)). That observation transfers to the llama.cpp cache discussed next; the engine does not. Verdict: skip colibri. The only scenario in which it becomes relevant is deliberately moving to a model that does not fit 376 GB of RAM (Kimi K3, GLM-5.x full), at which point its own tables show 0.05–3 tok/s on consumer hardware and multi-minute TTFT, which is unusable for a tool-calling loop.

## The llama.cpp expert cache is the one lever worth measuring, and it is VRAM-starved here

Upstream llama.cpp merged PR #29887 on 2026-10-07, adding `--moe-cache-mib N`: an LRU cache in VRAM for experts kept in host memory, where cache hits compute on the GPU and misses are uploaded over PCIe, applied only to batches of 32 tokens or fewer, with a recommended size of roughly 10% of total expert bytes; PR #30112 extended it across multiple GPUs on 2026-10-08 ([PR #29887](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/29887); [PR #30112](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/30112)). The maintainer's own table uses the agent's exact model at Q4_0 (93.7 GiB, of which 65.4 GiB experts): an RTX 4090 went from **25.0 to 40.7 tok/s (1.62x) with an 11 GB cache at 77% hit rate** and to 39.4 tok/s (1.57x) with a 6.5 GB cache at 72%, while an RTX 5090 with 19 GB reached 2.20x at 89% hit ([PR #29887](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/29887)). A local `grep` of `common/arg.cpp` at commit 1af554f8f returns no `moe-cache` symbol, so using it means `git pull` and a rebuild of `build-new`, after which the 24-day-old server process must be restarted and will pay a cold load.

The gap between those numbers and this box is VRAM and bus. The serve script records **14.7 to 17.4 GiB resident after KV, leaving ~3.3 GiB headroom on 21 GiB**, and that headroom was verified against `-ub 2048` at `-c 131072`, which "raises peak VRAM during prefill" ([oDA scripts/serve_qwen4exp.sh](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/scripts/serve_qwen4exp.sh)). Expert bytes for UD-Q4_K_XL are somewhat above the PR's 65.4 GiB, so the 10% guideline is ~7 GiB and the available budget is under half that, split across two cards. The PR states that "smaller cache sizes show diminishing returns on PCIe 3.0 hardware", the community RFC that preceded it reported "regressions on older GPUs" across a GTX 1080 Ti to 5090 test fleet, and one user saw GLM-5.3-Flash fall from 33 to 16 tok/s with an undersized cache at a 43.5% hit rate ([PR #29887](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/29887); [Discussion #24528](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/24528); [developersdigest](https://www.developersdigest.tech/blog/llama-cpp-moe-offload-n-cpu-moe-vs-moe-cache-2026)).

A back-of-envelope bound explains why hit rate is everything. With 512 experts across 48 layers, a Q4 expert is roughly 2.7 MB and a token touches 10 routed experts per layer, about 1.3 GB of expert weight per token; the papers note puts an achievable PCIe 3.0 x16 link at ~11–12 GB/s, so a token whose experts all miss spends ~110 ms on the bus, or ~9 tok/s, slower than today's 13.5 tok/s CPU path ([Fiddler, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2402.07033); [PR #29887](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/29887)). Break-even sits near a 40–50% hit rate; a 72% hit rate like the PR's smallest tested cache would give something like 1.2–1.5x, and the RFC author's framing is exact: the cache "converts a RAM-bandwidth problem into a PCIe-latency one" ([Discussion #24528](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/24528)). Two factors cut in the agent's favour. The workload is a single domain with a fixed 8.7k-token prefix, and MoE-Infinity's profiling found that for 100-plus-expert models "fewer than 5% of experts are repeatedly activated" within a request during decode, i.e. within-request skew is strong ([MoE-Infinity](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.14361v3)). And VRAM can be freed: the attention KV for the full 131,072 window is 3.00 GiB plus 0.46 GiB of recurrent state, yet the compaction trigger at 111,411 tokens has never fired and peak observed context was 75,571, so `-c 98304` or `-c 81920` returns roughly 0.75–1.15 GiB with no measured loss ([rtf local-frontier-inference.md](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/rtf-research/blob/main/docs/phases/phase-5-production-deepagents/local-frontier-inference.md); [oDA kvvram-full trace](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/docs/traces/2026-09-23_netbox-benchmark-v5_kvvram-full.md)). A 4–5 GiB cache is therefore reachable, but the honest prior is a modest gain or a wash, and the experiment must be run with `llama-bench` on the real model rather than assumed.

The research literature says not to look past that reactive LRU. ProMoE, the best-documented learned predictor, was implemented inside llama.cpp and gained **only 1.09x decode over a plain LRU cache there** versus 1.31x in HF transformers, because llama.cpp's baseline already overlaps well ([ProMoE](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.22134)). HOBBIT's ablation attributes most of its speedup to low-precision expert substitution, with prefetch worth ~10% and the cache policy 4.7–8.7% over LRU; Apple's SpecMD study concludes that "higher prediction accuracy does not guarantee better cache performance" ([HOBBIT](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.01433); [SpecMD](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.03921v1)). On Qwen3-30B-A3B, a clairvoyant oracle beats LRU by only 13 points of hit rate at 13% residency (79.1% vs 65.9%) ([Cacheable by Design](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18261)). None of these predictors ships in a mainstream engine, and porting one would buy at most tens of milliseconds per token on top of a cache that may not pay at all.

## AMX kernels, forks and research systems cannot run this model on this hardware

KTransformers is the production-grade alternative to llama.cpp for CPU-expert hybrids, and its numbers are real: 9.44x prefill and 3.03x decode over llama.cpp on DeepSeek-V3 ([KTransformers tutorial](https://github.com/kvcache-ai/ktransformers/blob/main/doc/en/DeepseekR1_V3_tutorial.md)). But those gains come from AMX kernels and NUMA weight duplication on Sapphire Rapids-class Xeons; the Xeon Silver 4210 is Cascade Lake with AVX-512 and VNNI but no AMX, so only the slower V0.2 path applies, no documentation establishes sm_75 (Turing) support, its model list does not include the `qwen4_exp` architecture, and the project's own ADR already recorded that "KTransformers requires Ampere+ and its headline numbers need AMX this Cascade Lake host lacks" ([ktransformers README](https://github.com/kvcache-ai/ktransformers); [rtf local-frontier-inference.md](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/rtf-research/blob/main/docs/phases/phase-5-production-deepagents/local-frontier-inference.md)). The same note disposes of vLLM and SGLang, which cannot host 112 GB of experts on 21 GiB of VRAM; vLLM's own expert-offload RFC remains unmerged ([vLLM Issue #38256](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/38256)).

ik_llama.cpp is nominally the power-user route and supports Turing, but its last upstream sync was August 2024, two years before Qwen3.8-Flash-Next's `qwen4_exp` architecture existed, and its measured advantage is on MLA models, with mainline "as fast or faster" on GQA MoE ([ik_llama.cpp README](https://github.com/ikawrakow/ik_llama.cpp); [ik-llama-toolkit](https://github.com/daimonionnn/ik-llama-toolkit/blob/main/README.md)). MoE-Infinity builds for sm_80/sm_90 only; PowerInfer supports only ReLU-family models with "no support for DeepSeek, Qwen3-MoE, or gpt-oss"; Fiddler runs Mixtral-8x7B fp16 only ([MoE-Infinity README](https://github.com/EfficientMoE/MoE-Infinity/blob/main/README.md); [PowerInfer README](https://github.com/Tiiny-AI/PowerInfer); [Fiddler README](https://github.com/efeslab/fiddler)). Ollama closed its own expert-offload PR unmerged and still cannot load this architecture at all ([Ollama PR #15207](https://github.com/ollama/ollama/pull/15207); [rtf ADR-0038](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/rtf-research/blob/main/docs/adr/0038-local-frontier-model-viable-cost-not-capability.md)).

Speculative decoding is the technique the literature rates highest for amortising expert loads at batch 1 (SpecMoEOff 2.5x, SP-MoE up to 3.5x TPOT), yet it is closed for this model: n-gram speculation measured **0 of 163 drafts accepted and decode fell 5.0 to 3.8 tok/s**, no draft model shares the `qwen4exp` vocabulary, the MTP head PRs #27836/#28243 are unmerged, and PR #29887 disables the expert cache under speculation anyway ([SpecMoEOff](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21706); [oDA scripts/serve_qwen4exp.sh](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/scripts/serve_qwen4exp.sh); [PR #29887](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/29887)). The MTP PRs are the one item here worth watching, since colibri reports MTP on Qwen3.8-Flash-Next at "+16–20%" and the decode side would then have a second lever ([colibri README](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri/blob/main/README.md)).

The NUMA angle is more interesting than the mirror PR suggests. The `--numa mirror` draft (PR #27986) turned out to be a steady-state wash for MoE decode once the baseline was corrected (16.4 vs 16.9 tok/s), but the same thread produced a finding that matters for prefill: GPU uploads must read from the replica local to the GPU's PCIe NUMA node, and a reviewer on a 2-socket Xeon saw **prefill halve from 900 to 500 tok/s when the GPU sat on socket 1** and reads came from the other node ([PR #27986](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/27986)). The agent runs `--numa isolate -t 10`, which confines threads and memory to whichever node the process happened to start on; which socket the two RTX 2080 Tis hang off is recorded nowhere in either repo, and every prefill microbatch uploads expert weights across PCIe. Checking `/sys/bus/pci/devices/<gpu>/numa_node` and launching llama-server under `numactl --cpunodebind=<that node> --membind=<that node>` costs nothing and is the only untested item in this review with a precedent for a large prefill effect. Alongside it, the author's own list still carries `sysctl kernel.numa_balancing=0`, which needs root and which llama.cpp warns about at startup ([oDA 2026-09-21 week-in-review](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/docs/development/2026-09-21_week-in-review-v5-benchmark-and-local-inference.md)).

## Agent-side changes have already outperformed every server flag

The measured history of this project is that application changes moved wall time more than inference changes. Rejecting the `__in` filter cut the session harness from **39 to 20 tool calls and from 1,552 s to 1,312 s at identical scores**; "on the clearest single case, one introspection replaced 19 MCP calls" ([rtf ADR-0038](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/rtf-research/blob/main/docs/adr/0038-local-frontier-model-viable-cost-not-capability.md)). By contrast, the biggest single inference win, `-ub 2048` at 3.8x isolated prefill, "became 1.23x end-to-end" because 85% of prompt tokens were already cached ([rtf RESEARCH_LOG 2026-09-21](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/rtf-research/blob/main/RESEARCH_LOG.md)). Three agent-side levers remain open and each targets a named cost in the profile.

Round trips first. Mean tool calls per question are 5.52, rising to 7.3 for advanced questions, and the same question can take 9 or 14 calls; the project's own planned remedy is "model-handoff routing — route simple queries to a cheap model, hard ones to a strong one", backed by the observation that cloud models answered the same 90 questions at **~34 s per item versus 271.6 s locally at statistically indistinguishable correctness (0.906 vs 0.911)** ([rtf ADR-0038](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/rtf-research/blob/main/docs/adr/0038-local-frontier-model-viable-cost-not-capability.md); [oDA 2026-09-21 week-in-review](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/docs/development/2026-09-21_week-in-review-v5-benchmark-and-local-inference.md)). Anything that saves one call saves 30–40 s, which is more than the expert cache's optimistic case saves across a whole turn.

Tool-result size second. The 167 s and 283 s calls were pure prefill of oversized MCP results; raw artifacts under `large_tool_results/` run from 81 KB to 439 KB with a median of 141 KB ([oDA 2026-09-28_web-chat.md](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/docs/development/2026-09-28_web-chat.md)). The one attempt to cap them in the MCP wrapper backfired into a 65-minute retry loop and was reverted ([rtf ADR-0038](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/rtf-research/blob/main/docs/adr/0038-local-frontier-model-viable-cost-not-capability.md)); the fix belongs in the tools themselves, returning fewer fields or paginating, where the GraphQL tool already lets the model select fields. At 130 tok/s every 10k characters of JSON the model never needed costs about 25 s.

Cold prefix third. Switching conversations drops the cache to the 8,719-token shared prefix and costs ~80 s on the next turn; llama-server's `--slot-save-path` save/restore exists in the local build and the project explicitly listed it as out of scope for the persistence work ([oDA docs/setup/web-chat.md](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/docs/setup/web-chat.md); [oDA PRPs/netbox-web-chat-persistence.md](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/PRPs/netbox-web-chat-persistence.md)). Saving a thread's slot on switch and restoring it on return turns an 80 s prefill into a disk read of a few GB. Shrinking the prefix by suppressing SubAgentMiddleware's `TASK_SYSTEM_PROMPT` is the smaller cousin of this item and also modestly helps decode, which slows 26% between 9k and 55k tokens of context ([oDA AGENTS.md](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/AGENTS.md); [oDA 2026-10-06 session trace](https://github.com/FinnMacCumail/ollamaDeepAgents/blob/master/docs/traces/2026-10-06_netbox-session_in-lookup-fix.md)).

## What to try, what to skip, and why

| 2 | Shrink tool results at the source (field selection, pagination in the MCP/GraphQL tools, not a wrapper cap) | 167–283 s prefill outliers | Removes the worst turns; ~25 s per 10k characters avoided | Tool code |

| Skip | ik_llama.cpp | — | Upstream sync August 2024; `qwen4_exp` absent; advantage is MLA-specific | — |

The explicit verdict on colibri is no. It is a serious engineering effort aimed at a different constraint, and on the one axis where it overlaps the agent's needs, RAM-resident MoE with a VRAM hot tier, llama.cpp is faster, more mature, better at tool calling and already running. Its real contribution to this project is the reminder, from its own routing-heat experiments, that a narrow workload makes small expert caches more valuable than general benchmarks suggest; that argument is the reason item 4 is worth running at all.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question. The agent's latency is not an expert-offloading problem that a better engine could solve; it is a serial-call-count problem running on top of an inference stack whose operator has already pulled every mainline lever and measured the dead ends. Expert offloading tooling, from colibri to the academic prefetchers, is almost entirely a decode story, and decode is at most half of a typical turn and none of the worst ones. The order of operations that follows from the measurements is unglamorous: remove calls, shrink what the model must read, make sure expert uploads are not crossing a socket boundary, and only then spend a bench session on the new GPU expert cache with realistic expectations.

Two things would change this verdict. If the MTP head for `qwen4exp` merges, speculative decoding becomes the first decode lever with literature support for amortising expert traffic on a bandwidth-bound box, and it should be tested the week it lands. And if the project ever moves to a model that does not fit 376 GB of RAM, colibri or llama.cpp's NVMe-streaming PR become relevant, at which point the measured 0.05–3 tok/s of disk-streamed decode means the interactive agent would need to be redesigned around a cheap-model-first routing policy regardless.
